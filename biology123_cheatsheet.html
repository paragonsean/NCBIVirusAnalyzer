Biology 123 — Master Cheatsheet

Reproduction • Transport • Gas Exchange
Shared Fundamentals Across the Unit
One logic: life runs on exchanges across surfaces. Every rate obeys Fick's Law: Rate = (A × ΔP) / D. Maximize surface area (A), keep the gradient (ΔP) high, minimize distance (D). Lungs, gills, tracheae, capillaries, stomata, and leaves all follow this rule. Transport systems exist because large bodies cannot rely on diffusion alone — as size grows, surface-area-to-volume ratio drops, so cells need help getting supplies and dumping wastes.
  • Diffusion: passive high → low concentration; no ATP.
  • Osmosis: water diffusion across a semipermeable membrane.
  • Gradient: difference that drives movement (pressure, concentration, water potential).
  • SA : V ratio: small organisms diffuse directly; large ones evolve transport.
  • Mitosis vs. meiosis: identical diploids (asexual) vs. recombined haploids (sexual).
  • Gamete: haploid sex cell (sperm, egg, pollen, ovule).
  • Zygote: diploid cell formed at fertilization.
  • Haploid / Diploid: 1 vs. 2 sets of chromosomes (n vs. 2n).
  • Cohesion / Adhesion: water-water / water-wall — drives xylem.
  • Source / Sink: sugar producer (leaf) / sugar user (root, fruit, seed).
  • Endotherm / Ectotherm: generates / absorbs heat; endotherms demand more O2.
  • ATP: cellular energy currency made by respiration.
  • Hypotonic / Hypertonic / Isotonic: lower / higher / equal solute than cell.
  • Active vs. Passive transport: with ATP vs. down a gradient for free.
1 • Animal Asexual Reproduction
Core idea: one parent → genetically identical offspring by mitosis. Fast, cheap, and reliable in stable environments — but zero genetic variation, so a single pathogen or climate shift can wipe out the whole population. Most often seen in simple animals and in good conditions where rapid population growth is favored.

A. Fission (Binary Fission)

  • Parent splits into two roughly equal parts; each regenerates missing structures.
  • Examples: planaria flatworms, sea anemones, protists, many bacteria.

B. Budding

  • A small outgrowth grows into a new individual; bud is smaller than parent.
  • May detach, or stay attached to form a colony (Hydra, corals, sponges).

C. Fragmentation

  • A piece of the parent breaks off and regenerates into a whole organism.
  • Examples: sea stars, sponges, ribbon worms. Small fragments may form cysts.

D. Parthenogenesis

  • Unfertilized egg develops into a new individual with no sperm.
  • Obligate: whiptail lizards (all-female species).
  • Facultative: Komodo dragons, some sharks when isolated.
  • Social insects: bees — unfertilized → haploid male drones; fertilized → diploid females.

Why asexual works

  • No need to find a mate; all offspring can reproduce.
  • Best when conditions are stable and resources are plentiful.
  • Many species switch: asexual in good times, sexual under stress.

2 • Sexual Reproduction & Plant Reproduction
Sex Determination & Hermaphroditism
  • Gonochoristic: sex fixed at birth — most vertebrates, separate males and females.
  • Simultaneous hermaphrodites: both organs at once (earthworms, banana slugs); usually cross-fertilize.
  • Sequential hermaphrodites: switch sex during life based on age or social cues.
    • Protandrous: male → female (clownfish).
    • Protogynous: female → male (blue-headed wrasse).
  • Self-fertilization: rare — some tapeworms; reduces variation.

Embryonic Sex Determination

  • Genetic (GSD): chromosomes decide — XX/XY humans, ZW birds, XO insects.
  • Temperature (TSD): incubation temperature sets sex — turtles, crocodilians.
  • Environmental: density, pH, hormones, social cues.

r vs. K Selection Strategies

  • r-selected: many small offspring, little care, high mortality (fish, insects, frogs).
  • K-selected: few large offspring, heavy care (mammals, birds, elephants).
  • Most species sit on a spectrum between these extremes.
Fertilization & Developmental Modes
  • External fertilization: in water — fish, amphibians, coral. Requires synchrony (spawning); huge gamete numbers offset loss.
  • Internal fertilization: sperm delivered inside female — higher success, fewer offspring; needs courtship and copulatory organs (penis, claspers) or spermatophores (salamanders).

Developmental Modes

  • Oviparous: eggs laid externally (birds, most reptiles, insects).
  • Ovoviviparous: eggs retained inside mother, fed by yolk only (some sharks, guppies).
  • Viviparous: embryo fed internally by placenta (most mammals).

Mammal Groups

  • Monotremes: egg-laying mammals — platypus, echidna.
  • Marsupials: short gestation + long pouch development (kangaroo, opossum).
  • Placental (eutherian): longest gestation; highest parental investment.

Reproductive Cycles

  • Estrous cycle: unused uterine lining reabsorbed; most mammals.
  • Menstrual cycle: uterine lining shed externally; primates.
  • Delayed implantation: fertilized egg pauses until conditions right (bears, some bats).
  • Diapause: embryonic development pauses during stress.

Double Fertilization (angiosperm hallmark)

  • Sperm 1 + Egg → Zygote (2n) → embryo.
  • Sperm 2 + 2 polar nuclei → Endosperm (3n) food tissue.
  • Ensures food tissue made only when fertilization succeeds.
  • Seed: dormant embryo + food + protective coat.
  • Fruit: mature ovary — aids seed dispersal (wind, water, animals).
  • Pericarp layers: exocarp (skin), mesocarp (flesh), endocarp (pit).

Plant Asexual / Vegetative Reproduction

  • Apomixis: clonal seed from mitosis — no fertilization.
  • Runners (stolons): strawberry — horizontal stems root at nodes.
  • Rhizomes / tubers: ginger / potato — underground stems.
  • Suckers / plantlets: raspberry / Mother of Thousands.
  • Grafting & cuttings: human-assisted clonal propagation.
3 • Animal Transport & Human Circulation
Large bodies can't rely on diffusion — a circulatory system bulk-moves fluid so every cell is close to an exchange surface. The heart is the pump, vessels are the plumbing, and blood carries O2, CO2, nutrients, waste, hormones, and heat.
Circulatory System Types & Vertebrate Hearts
  • Open circulation: hemolymph bathes organs directly; low pressure. Most arthropods and mollusks (except cephalopods).
  • Closed circulation: blood stays in vessels; heart pumps under pressure. Vertebrates, annelids, octopus/squid.
  • Closed systems support higher metabolism, finer control, and more oxygen delivery.

Vertebrate Heart Evolution

  • Fish: 2 chambers — single circulation; blood loses pressure at gills.
  • Amphibians: 3 chambers (2 atria, 1 ventricle); some mixing.
  • Most reptiles: 3 chambers with partial septum; can shunt blood (diving turtles).
  • Crocodilians: 4 chambers but with a mixing vessel.
  • Birds & Mammals: 4 chambers, full separation — supports endothermy and high activity.

Single vs. Double Circulation

  • Single: blood through heart once per lap (fish).
  • Double: pulmonary (to lungs) + systemic (to body) — much higher pressure.
Human Heart — Cardiac Cycle & Conduction

Cardiac Cycle Phases

  • Systole: contraction — blood ejected from chambers.
  • Diastole: relaxation — chambers fill.
  • AV valves (tricuspid, mitral): close during systole = "lub."
  • Semilunar valves (aortic, pulmonary): close at end of systole = "dub."

Electrical Conduction

  • SA node (pacemaker, right atrium) fires → atria contract.
  • AV node delays signal ~0.1 s to let atria empty.
  • Signal races down Bundle of His → Purkinje fibers → ventricles contract bottom up.
  • Stroke volume (SV): volume per beat — ~70 mL.
  • Heart rate (HR): beats per minute — ~70 at rest.
  • Cardiac output (CO): HR × SV ≈ 5 L/min — all your blood every minute.
  • ECG/EKG: P = atrial depolarization, QRS = ventricular depolarization, T = ventricular repolarization.
  • Blood pressure: systolic/diastolic (e.g. 120/80 mmHg). Hypertension ≥ 140/90.
  • Baroreceptors: pressure sensors in aorta/carotid → autonomic + hormonal adjust.
  • Lymphatic system: returns leaked plasma (~3 L/day); failure → edema.
Blood Vessels, Blood Cells & Gas Carriage

Vessels

  • Arteries: thick muscular walls; carry blood away from heart under high pressure.
  • Arterioles: regulate flow into capillaries (vasoconstriction/dilation).
  • Capillaries: one cell thick — the actual exchange site.
  • Venules / Veins: thinner walls, lower pressure, valves prevent backflow.

Blood Composition

  • Plasma (~55%): water, proteins, ions, nutrients, wastes, hormones.
  • Red blood cells: carry O2 via hemoglobin; no nucleus in mammals.
  • White blood cells: immune defense.
  • Platelets: cell fragments that clot bleeding.
  • O2 transport: ~98% bound to hemoglobin; ~2% dissolved. Each Hb carries up to 4 O2.
  • CO2 transport: ~70% as bicarbonate (HCO3-); ~23% on Hb; ~7% dissolved.
  • Bohr effect: low pH / high CO2 / high temp → Hb releases O2 where tissues need it.
  • Myoglobin: single-unit O2 reserve in muscle; critical in diving mammals.
  • Respiratory pigments: Hemoglobin (Fe, red) — vertebrates; Hemocyanin (Cu, blue) — mollusks/arthropods; Hemerythrin (Fe, violet) — some worms.
  • Clotting cascade: platelets + fibrinogen → fibrin mesh → scab.
4 • Plant Transport & Stress Responses
Xylem moves water and dissolved minerals up from soil to leaves; phloem moves sugars from source to sink. Most plant transport is passive — powered by evaporation (transpiration) and osmotic gradients generated by active sugar loading. Plants have no pump; they rely on the physics of water.

Mechanisms of Movement

Transpiration–Cohesion–Tension (xylem)

  • Evaporation at stomata creates negative pressure (tension).
  • Cohesion: water-to-water H-bonds keep the column intact.
  • Adhesion: water sticks to xylem walls — helps hold column up.
  • Water column is pulled up like a straw — root hair to leaf.
  • Explains how redwoods (100+ m) lift water against gravity.

Water Potential (ψ)

  • Water moves high ψ → low ψ.
  • Turgid: swollen, healthy posture.
  • Flaccid: wilting.
  • Plasmolysis: membrane pulls from wall in hypertonic solution.

Pressure-Flow Hypothesis (phloem)

  • At source (mature leaf), sugar is actively loaded into sieve tubes.
  • Water follows by osmosis → high pressure at source.
  • At sink (root, fruit), sugar unloaded; water leaves → low pressure.
  • Sap flows high → low pressure, carrying sugars.
  • Source/sink roles can switch seasonally.

Root Uptake

  • Root hairs: increase surface area.
  • Endodermis: innermost root cortex layer — the "bouncer."
  • Casparian strip: waxy suberin band forcing water through membranes for selective mineral uptake.
  • Mycorrhizae: fungal root partners boost water/phosphorus uptake.
Plant Stress Responses

Drought / Heat

  • Cavitation: air bubble breaks xylem column — disastrous.
  • Stomatal pits & trichomes: trap humid air, block drying wind.
  • Thick cuticle: waxy waterproof leaf coating.
  • CAM photosynthesis: stomata open at night — cacti, succulents, pineapple.
  • C4 photosynthesis: efficient in hot/bright — corn, sugarcane.
  • Leaf drop (deciduous): shed leaves in drought/cold.

Flooding / Low Oxygen

  • Lenticels: spongy pores in bark allow gas exchange when wet.
  • Aerenchyma: air-filled tissue piping O2 to submerged roots (rice, water lilies).
  • Pneumatophores: "knee" roots above water (mangroves, cypress).
  • Adventitious roots: sprout from stems under stress.

Cold & Salt

  • Antifreeze solutes: prevent ice damage.
  • Salt glands: mangroves secrete excess salt from leaves.
5 • Gas Exchange Fundamentals & Animal Systems
Rate = (A × ΔP) / D. Every exchange structure — gills, lungs, tracheae, alveoli, skin — maximizes surface area, maintains the gradient by ventilating and circulating, and keeps distance tiny by being one cell thick and always moist (gases must dissolve to diffuse).
Key Vocabulary & Ventilation Methods
  • Gas exchange: passive diffusion of O2 in and CO2 out across a moist surface.
  • Respiration — 3 meanings: External (breathing), internal (capillary-to-cell), cellular (ATP production).
  • Aqueous requirement: surfaces must stay moist.
  • Partial pressure: pressure of one gas in a mixture — drives diffusion.
  • Counter- vs. co-current: flow direction determines efficiency.

Ventilation Methods

  • Buccal pumping: mouth/throat gulp (fish, amphibians).
  • Ram ventilation: swim fast with mouth open (tuna, sharks).
  • Costal ventilation: ribs expand chest — negative pressure pulls air (reptiles, mammals).
  • Tidal ventilation: air in/out same path — mammals; residual volume limits efficiency.
  • Countercurrent (fish gills): blood opposite to water → ~80–90% O2 extraction.
  • Cross-current (birds): one-way airflow through parabronchi; best at altitude.

Human Breathing Control

What controls breathing?

  • Rate is set mainly by CO2 / pH, NOT O2.
  • Holding your breath fails because rising CO2 → falling pH triggers urge to breathe.
  • Chemoreceptors in medulla, aorta, carotid sense blood CO2/pH.
  • Medulla oblongata signals diaphragm + intercostals.

Breathing Mechanics

  • Inhale: diaphragm contracts, ribs lift → chest volume ↑ → pressure ↓ → air in.
  • Exhale: diaphragm relaxes, volume ↓ → pressure ↑ → air out (passive at rest).
  • Bohr effect makes O2 unload automatically at hard-working muscles.
  • Residual volume: lungs never fully empty.
6 • Plant Gas Exchange, Leaves & Unit Summary
Plants need CO2 in for photosynthesis (day), plus O2 in for cellular respiration (always, but net uptake at night), and O2 out during the day. All exchange is passive diffusion through specialized pores — stomata in leaves, lenticels in woody bark.
Stomata, Guard Cells & Leaf Tissues
  • Stomata: pores (usually leaf underside); sole path for CO2 in.
  • Guard cells: kidney-shaped pair flanking each stoma; open/close by turgor driven by K+ and water.
    • Moist: turgid → open.
    • Heat/drought: flaccid → closed.
  • Cuticle: waxy waterproof layer — forces exchange through stomata.
  • Lenticels: raised pores in bark of woody plants.
  • Epidermis: protective single cell layer top and bottom; transparent.

Mesophyll (middle of leaf)

  • Palisade mesophyll: tightly packed upper layer — main photosynthesis site.
  • Spongy mesophyll: loose lower layer — air-filled; gases diffuse to every cell.

CAM Photosynthesis (water savers)

  • Stomata open at night — CO2 stored as malic acid in vacuoles.
  • Closed by day — saves water; stored CO2 fuels photosynthesis.
  • Examples: cacti, pineapples, succulents, agave.

C4 Photosynthesis

  • Spatial split: CO2 fixed in mesophyll, Calvin cycle in bundle sheath.
  • Efficient in hot/bright conditions — corn, sugarcane.

Unit Summary — Patterns to Remember
  • Exchange obeys Fick's Law: maximize A, maintain ΔP, minimize D — gills, lungs, alveoli, capillaries, stomata, leaves.
  • Water is universal: gas exchange needs moist surfaces; plant transport runs on water columns; fertilization often happens in water.
  • Cost drives strategy: asexual is cheap but inflexible; sexual is expensive but adaptive. Open circulation is cheap; closed is powerful. Diffusion is free; active transport costs ATP.
  • Variation wins long-term: meiosis, double fertilization, seed dispersal — all deliver variation.
  • Structure fits function: alveoli tiny/many; lamellae stacked; capillaries one cell thick; leaves flat; xylem cells dead hollow tubes — geometry always serves the job.
